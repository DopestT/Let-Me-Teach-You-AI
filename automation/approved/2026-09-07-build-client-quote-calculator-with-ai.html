Build a Client Quote Calculator With AI

Turn your pricing rules into a useful interactive tool clients can actually use.

A potential client asks, “How much would this cost?”

You could send them a paragraph, a PDF, or a price list. Or you could give them a simple calculator that asks what they need and returns a realistic estimate.

That used to sound like a small software project. It increasingly does not.

Canva says Canva Code 2.0 can build websites, apps, landing pages, calculators and other interactive experiences from conversational instructions, with responsive output and visual editing. Canva also says the current version can start from a prompt, template, or imported HTML. That makes a quote calculator a useful beginner project: it has a clear input, a clear output, and a real business purpose.

Why it matters

The important change is not that AI can “write code.” The useful change is that a non-developer can now prototype a small piece of business software without first learning a programming stack.

A quote calculator can help a freelancer, consultant, cleaner, photographer, designer, tutor, event professional, lawn-care operator, or other service business turn vague pricing conversations into a repeatable intake experience.

It can also expose a problem before you publish anything: if you cannot explain your own pricing rules clearly enough for a calculator, your customers probably cannot understand them either.

This is not a promise that a calculator will increase sales. It is a way to make an offer clearer, reduce repetitive back-and-forth, and test a more useful customer experience.

The simple version

A quote calculator is just four things:

  1. Inputs — what the customer chooses or enters.
  2. Rules — how those choices affect the estimate.
  3. Output — the estimated price or range.
  4. Next step — what the customer should do after seeing it.

Example: a freelance video editor charges a $150 base project fee, $75 per finished minute, and $50 for 48-hour rush delivery.

$150 base + (3 × $75) + $50 rush = $425.

Version one only needs to calculate correctly and explain that the result is an estimate.

Key terms

Input: Information a user gives the calculator, such as quantity, package, duration, or add-ons.

Pricing rule: The math or condition that changes the estimate.

Validation: Testing whether the calculator produces the correct result for known examples.

Edge case: An unusual input that can break your logic, such as zero quantity, a negative number, or an unsupported combination.

Build it: the workflow

  1. Choose one service, not your whole business. Start with the offer you explain most often.
  2. Write the pricing rules without AI. List the base price, units, add-ons, minimums, maximums, taxes if applicable, discounts you actually offer, and anything that requires a custom quote. Do not ask AI to invent these numbers.
  3. Create five test quotes by hand. Include a normal order, smallest allowed order, larger order, an add-on, and one custom-quote case.
  4. Decide what the customer should see. Ask only for information needed to calculate the estimate.
  5. Give the builder the rules and interface together. Open Canva Code or another AI coding/design tool and describe the calculator in plain language.
  6. Require transparent math. Show the estimate breakdown, not just the final number.
  7. Add guardrails. Prevent negative quantities, set sensible limits, and route complicated combinations to a custom-quote message.
  8. Test against your five hand-calculated examples. If even one answer is wrong, do not publish.
  9. Test the experience on a phone. Check tap targets, long labels, price formatting, and readability.
  10. Publish the smallest useful version. Give it one clear next action: request a firm quote, book a consultation, email you, or continue to your service page.
Copy-ready build prompt
Build a clean, mobile-friendly quote calculator for my [SERVICE]. Use ONLY the pricing rules below. Do not invent prices, discounts, fees, policies, or services. PRICING RULES Base price: [BASE PRICE] Unit price: [PRICE + UNIT] Minimum: [MINIMUM OR NONE] Maximum before custom quote: [MAXIMUM OR NONE] Package options: [OPTIONS + EXACT PRICE EFFECT] Add-ons: [ADD-ONS + EXACT PRICE EFFECT] Rush fee: [RULE OR NONE] Other verified rules: [RULES] CUSTOMER INPUTS Ask for only: [INPUTS]. OUTPUT Show: 1. estimated total, 2. a line-by-line breakdown of the math, 3. the label “Estimate only—not a final invoice,” 4. a clear [REQUEST QUOTE / BOOK / CONTACT] button. GUARDRAILS Reject impossible or negative values. If an input falls outside these rules, do not guess. Display: “This needs a custom quote.” Do not collect sensitive personal information. DESIGN Use [BRAND COLORS / STYLE]. Keep the interface simple enough to use comfortably on a phone. TEST CASES The calculator must produce these exact expected results: [TEST 1] [TEST 2] [TEST 3] [TEST 4] [TEST 5] Before presenting the final version, check the calculation logic against every test case and tell me whether each one passes.

Privacy + safety

Do not put private customer records, passwords, payment-card data, Social Security numbers, medical information, confidential contracts, or other sensitive data into a prototype just because the interface makes it easy to add fields.

Generated software still needs human testing. AI can produce plausible-looking logic that is wrong. Your hand-calculated test cases—not the appearance of the page—decide whether the calculator is ready.

If your pricing involves regulated financial, legal, medical, insurance, tax, lending, or other high-stakes decisions, a generic AI-built estimator is not a substitute for appropriate professional and compliance review.

Try this today

Take the service you quoted most recently and write down the exact math you used.

Customer chooses: ______
Price changes by: ______
Final estimate shows: ______

If those three lines are clear, you already have the skeleton of your first calculator.

Common mistakes

  • Building before defining the prices.
  • Testing only one happy path.
  • Hiding the calculation.
  • Collecting too much information.
  • Calling an estimate a guaranteed price.
  • Overbuilding version one.

Final takeaway

AI coding becomes much more useful when you stop asking, “What app should I build?” and start asking, “What repetitive decision in my real work can I turn into a small tool?”

A quote calculator is a strong first answer because you already know the business logic. AI’s job is to turn that logic into an interface. Your job is to supply the truth, test the math, and decide whether the result is good enough for a customer.
